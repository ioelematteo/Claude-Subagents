# Content Strategy — Short-Form Vertical Video Channel: Personal Finance for College Students

## 1. Content Pillars

1. **Money basics for beginners** — foundational topics explained from zero: budgeting, bank accounts, credit scores, debt, taxes on student income.
2. **Student-specific money decisions** — choices unique to the college years: meal plans vs. cooking, textbooks, housing, part-time work, splitting costs with roommates.
3. **Saving and first investing steps** — building an emergency buffer, saving goals, and the very first steps toward investing, framed as long-horizon habits rather than trades.

## 2. Target Viewer

College students and recent graduates, roughly 18–24, who are financially responsible for themselves for the first time or about to be. They earn little or nothing, have limited or no prior financial education, learn primarily through short-form video, and want practical steps they can apply this week. They are skeptical of finance content that feels like selling.

## 3. Tone of Voice

Direct, plain-spoken, non-judgmental, lightly humorous. No jargon without a one-line explanation. No hype, no fear-based framing, no promises of returns or wealth. Peer-to-peer rather than expert-to-student: "here is what this means and what you can do next."

## 4. Video Topics, Ranked by Expected ROI

1. **"How to build a budget when your income is irregular"** — Assumption: demand is high because irregular income (part-time shifts, gig work, seasonal jobs) is the common student reality and mainstream budgeting content assumes a fixed salary. Assumption: competition is moderate, as most budgeting videos target salaried adults rather than students. Assumption: RPM potential is mid-to-high, since budgeting content attracts advertisers in banking and budgeting apps.
2. **"What a credit score is and the three habits that move it"** — Assumption: demand is high and durable, because credit is a near-universal first financial question for this age group. Assumption: competition is high, with many established creators in this space, so differentiation must come from the student framing. Assumption: RPM potential is high, as credit-related topics draw financial-product advertisers.
3. **"Splitting rent, groceries and bills with roommates without conflict"** — Assumption: demand is steady and seasonal, peaking around lease-signing and move-in periods. Assumption: competition is low in personal finance channels but exists in general roommate/lifestyle content. Assumption: RPM potential is lower, because the topic is adjacent to finance and attracts fewer financial advertisers.
